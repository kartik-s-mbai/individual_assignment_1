### Q1. Who are your users, and what changes for them?

The users I had in mind for SneakerFreaks are male and female sneaker fans between the ages of 18 and 35, mainly in the United States but also potentially anywhere in the world. Some may be collectors, while others may just really like retro style sneakers and want to keep track of pairs they are interested in. Normally, they would have to look through different sneaker websites, open several pages, compare things like the name, colorway, release year, price, and availability, and then remember which pairs they liked or save them somewhere else.

My app puts that process into three screens. The catalog lets them browse the sneakers and see the main details. They can then open a sneaker to see more information about it. If they like it, they can add it to My Collection. The main change is that browsing, looking at details, and keeping track of the sneakers they like can all happen in one place instead of across several websites or tabs.

### Q2. Augmented capacity and constrained capacity

The biggest increase in my capacity was being able to build a working React app even though I do not know React. Before this assignment, I would not have been able to create three connected screens, fill them with data, and make the app work on a phone in the amount of time we had. Instead of spending most of my time trying to learn how to code everything, I could focus on what I actually wanted SneakerFreaks to do, what information should be shown, and whether the finished app matched the prompt I wrote. In that sense, I was making most of the product decisions while AI Studio was doing most of the technical work.

The main constraint was that I could tell whether the app worked, but I could not really tell whether the code behind it was good. I could test the catalog, sneaker detail screen, and My Collection, but I could not read hundreds of lines of React and know if something was written badly. I also noticed that anything I did not clearly describe became the builder's choice. Things like the exact layout, spacing, button placement, and overall look were mostly decided for me. That saved a lot of time, but it also meant parts of the final app came from the model rather than from choices I made myself.

### Q3. In the loop, on the loop, out of the loop: where was your judgment actually needed?

My judgment mattered most when I was deciding what SneakerFreaks should actually be. One example was changing the idea from using real retro sneaker brands to fully fictional sneakers because the assignment said not to use real company names, logos, or trademarks. That changed the product itself, not just the wording of the prompt. I was clearly in the loop there because I made a decision the builder could not make for me.

On the other hand, I was mostly just on the loop for things like the exact layout, spacing, and button placement. I looked at the result and accepted most of it because it worked, but I was not really making those design decisions myself.

If SneakerFreaks were a real product, I would be comfortable leaving basic filtering, navigation, and moving between screens out of the loop because those actions are easy to undo and low risk. I would keep a human involved in checking any real price or availability information before it was shown to users. If that information were wrong, users could make decisions based on something that was not true, so I would want that checked before it went live.

### Q4. What did it build that you never sketched?

The biggest thing AI Studio built that I never really sketched was the exact look of the app. In my prompt, I described what each screen needed to show and what the user should be able to do, but I did not draw out where every item should go. The builder decided how the sneaker cards would be arranged, where the buttons would sit, how the screens would connect, and how the app would look on a phone.

I noticed this during the build because the first working version already looked much more complete than what I had pictured in my head. I had focused more on the function of the app than the design of each screen. Looking back, I realized that not specifying those details meant I was letting the model make a lot of product decisions for me. If I wanted more control, I would have needed to sketch each screen more clearly or describe the layout and placement of the main parts before starting the build.

### Q5. Learning pointers for the organisational context

If a company was going to let a lot of employees build apps this way, my first **pointer** would be to make people write a clear prompt before they start, because in my SneakerFreaks build the model made decisions for me whenever I left something vague.

My second **pointer** would be to have someone with actual coding knowledge review the app before it is used for anything important, because I could tell whether my catalog, detail page, and collection worked, but I could not really judge the quality of the React code behind them.

My third **pointer** would be to keep the original prompt and a simple record of what the app does and which files it uses, because AI Studio built my app very quickly and it would be hard for someone else to take over later if all they had was the finished product.

The main thing I took away from this exercise is that the quality of the app depended a lot on how clearly I explained what I wanted before the model started building it, which basically comes down to the quality of the prompt.
